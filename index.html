How the calculation works
  1. Keep: the balance runs down month by month at the customer rate (or the rate after the cut). Each month earns the rate minus your funding cost on the balance. Expected loss and any fee waiver are taken off, plus a credit decision cost when the offer is a refinancing or top-up. A rate or fee change is an amendment and usually needs no new credit decision (check local rules).
  2. Let go: the repaid balance is lent out again after the delay you set and earns the risk-adjusted margin on new loans, running down over the rest of the term. The unused part of upfront fees is refunded, shown here pro rata to the remaining term (the exact method varies by market). Compensation is what you enter, limited to the legal cap (1% of the balance if more than 12 months remain, 0.5% otherwise) and never more than the interest still to come.
  3. Letting go assumes the repaid cash really is lent out again at the margin you enter, and that this new lending would not have happened anyway. If you are limited by demand or by credit appetite rather than by funding, the value of letting go is lower: closer to 0 margin.
  4. Rules: since the Lexitor ruling (2019) early repayment reduces all costs, upfront fees included. Compensation has been capped since 2008 (Directive 2008/48/EC, Art. 16), fixed rate only, and national rules add conditions on when it applies, for example in Latvia and Lithuania. CCD II (Directive (EU) 2023/2225) codifies this for new contracts from 20 November 2026; existing contracts generally stay under the old rules.
  5. Both sides are net present values (NPV): every monthly amount is discounted to today at your discount rate. The balance itself comes back in both cases, so only what differs is counted: margin, loss, fees, compensation and the offer. When market rates move, the margin on new loans and your funding cost move too: rerun the decision.
  6. Break-even offer: the value of keeping before any offer, minus the value of letting go. Any offer costing more than this loses money. It is a ceiling: the best offer is usually lower, the one that keeps the most expected value.
  7. Across customers: real leavers who accept add the gap between keeping and letting go. Customers who would have stayed anyway take the offer too, so their share costs the full offer.
  8. The commission write-off is shown only in the P&L view: in cash terms it is already spent.
One possible approach, kept simple on purpose. A full solution runs a decision engine across the whole lifecycle, not only at the payoff request: it makes the better-economics offer before the customer starts looking elsewhere. Portfolio modelling, segment models, live pricing and channel design complete it. Example values are illustrative, not data from any lender. Not financial or legal advice.
